The lighthouse keeper had a habit of counting the gulls each morning, though the number never seemed to matter to anyone but him. Some days there were forty-two, other days only nine, and once, during a strange October fog, there had been none at all. He wrote each count in a small green notebook that he kept beside the kettle, and over the years the notebook had grown thick with numbers, coffee rings, and the occasional pressed flower that had blown in through the window.

Down in the village, the bakery opened at six. The baker, a tall woman with flour permanently settled into the creases of her knuckles, believed that bread tasted better when it was spoken to. She told the sourdough about the weather and the rye about her sister's wedding plans. The croissants, she claimed, preferred silence. Nobody argued with her, mostly because the croissants really were the best for fifty miles in any direction.

On Tuesdays a travelling bookseller parked his van near the harbour. His stock changed constantly and followed no system anyone could identify. A cookbook for volcanic regions might sit beside a manual on repairing accordions, which leaned against a slim poetry collection written entirely about doorways. Children liked to guess what he would bring next week, and the guesses were usually wrong in delightful ways.

The river that ran through the valley had once been used to power a mill, but the wheel had stopped turning decades ago. Moss covered its spokes now, and a family of otters had claimed the space beneath it. Walkers would pause on the stone bridge to watch them, and some swore the otters performed for an audience, tumbling and diving with more flair whenever a crowd gathered.

Further up the hill stood an observatory that had been built by an eccentric clockmaker in the eighteen-hundreds. He had wanted to measure time by the stars rather than by gears, convinced that mechanical clocks were always slightly lying. The telescope still worked, and on clear nights a volunteer astronomy club gathered to peer at Saturn's rings, arguing gently about whether the planet looked more like a hat or a spinning plate.

Autumn arrived in the valley all at once, as if someone had flipped a switch. One afternoon the trees were green, and the next they had turned the colour of marmalade and rust. The air smelled of woodsmoke and wet leaves, and the market stalls began selling roasted chestnuts in paper cones that warmed your hands better than any gloves could.

As the evenings drew in, the lighthouse keeper added a new column to his notebook. Beside the count of gulls, he began recording the colour of the sunset: amber, violet, a pale bruised pink. He wasn't sure why. Perhaps, he thought, someone years from now would find the notebook and understand something about this place that couldn't be said any other way.
